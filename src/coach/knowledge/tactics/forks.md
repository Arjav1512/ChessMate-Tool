# Forks

A fork is one piece attacking two or more targets at once. The opponent saves one; you take the other.

**Why it matters:** Forks are the most common tactic in amateur chess by a wide margin. Most "I just lost a piece" moments are knight forks that were visible one move earlier.

**Recognize it:** Learn the geometry: knights fork targets that sit on the same color complex in an L-pattern (king on g8 + queen on d8 → fork square e7 or f6); pawns fork pieces two files apart on the same rank; queens fork along crossing lines, typically a check plus a loose piece.

**Common mistakes:**
- Parking king and queen (or king and rook) on knight-forkable squares — c2/c7 and f2/f7 are the classics.
- Seeing your own forks but not preventing the opponent's — check EVERY enemy knight's next two hops.
- Forcing a fork that wins two pawns while wrecking your position; tactics serve the position, not the other way around.

**How to think:** Forks are built, not found: first ask "which two enemy pieces stand fork-compatible?", then "what forcing move (check, capture, threat) drives them there or clears my path?" Luring a king or queen onto the fork square with a sacrifice is the standard two-move recipe.

**Example:** The eternal club classic: an enemy king still on e8, a rook stranded on a8, and your knight arriving on c7 — Nc7+ forks them the moment the d8-queen no longer guards c7. This is exactly why Nb5 punishes early queen sorties like the Sicilian's ...Qb6xb2: the queen leaves home, and c7 stops being defended.

**Coach's tip:** Before every move, glance at each enemy knight and ask: "if it moved twice, could it hit two of my pieces?" That single habit deletes a class of losses.

**Train it:** Do 20 knight-fork puzzles, then play a few games where, on every opponent knight move, you name its next two possible hops aloud (or in your head).
