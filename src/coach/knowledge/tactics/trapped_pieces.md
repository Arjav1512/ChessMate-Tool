# Trapped Pieces

A piece with no safe squares is material waiting to be collected. Trapping wins pieces without tactics fireworks — just a slow closing of doors.

**Why it matters:** Amateur games are full of pieces that wandered one square too far (the b2/g2-pawn-grabbing queen, the h-file knight, the a-file bishop). Recognizing shrinking mobility — yours and theirs — converts directly into material.

**Recognize it:** Watch for pieces whose escape squares can be counted on one hand: a knight on the rim, a bishop that grabbed a wing pawn, a queen deep in enemy territory. When the count hits two, start hunting; when it hits zero, harvest.

**Common mistakes:**
- Sending the queen pawn-hunting into a cage (the Poisoned Pawn on b2 is theory precisely because the cage almost closes).
- Winning a trapped piece too eagerly — sometimes besieging it while it watches costs the opponent more than capturing it.
- Trapping a piece at the cost of your position: if freeing it costs them two tempi, that might already be enough profit.

**How to think:** For each far-advanced piece (either side), list its legal retreats after the opponent's best restricting move. Trap-building is usually quiet: pawn moves that steal squares, a bishop that covers the escape diagonal. Only when the net is complete does the capture matter.

**Example:** The classic bishop trap: 1.d4 d5 2.c4 dxc4 3.e3 b5? 4.a4 c6 5.axb5 cxb5 6.Qf3! — the a8-rook and the whole queenside cave in; similarly, the Noah's Ark trap in the Ruy Lopez (…c5–c4 netting the b3-bishop) shows even grandmaster pieces drown on open boards.

**Coach's tip:** Every time any piece — yours or theirs — crosses the fourth rank or grabs a wing pawn, ask one question: "what is its route home?" No route, no trip.

**Train it:** Play a training game where your only goal is to reduce one enemy piece to zero moves. You'll be surprised how often normal moves accomplish it.
