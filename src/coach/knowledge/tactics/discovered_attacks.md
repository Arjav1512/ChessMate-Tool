# Discovered Attacks

A discovered attack unleashes a hidden line piece by moving the piece in front of it — two attacks from one move, and the moving piece may create a third threat of its own.

**Why it matters:** Discoveries are chess's closest thing to a free move: the opponent can usually parry only one of the two threats. Discovered checks are the strongest tactic in the game — the moving piece can take almost anything with impunity.

**Recognize it:** Look for batteries-in-waiting: your bishop/rook/queen on a line with exactly ONE of your own pieces between it and an enemy target. That front piece is a loaded spring. Equally: notice when an enemy piece stands suspiciously "passive" behind its own knight — you may be the target.

**Common mistakes:**
- Capturing the front piece of an enemy battery and "discovering" their attack for them.
- Spending the discovery cheaply: with a discovered check, the front piece can usually grab the biggest target, not the nearest.
- Missing the defensive resource: blocking the rear piece's line often kills both threats at once.

**How to think:** When you have a potential discovery, ask two questions: what is the BEST square for the front piece (maximum threat), and must the rear piece's target stay put? Sequence matters: often you first force the enemy king or queen onto the discovery line, then release the spring.

**Example:** 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Nd4? 4.Nxe5! — if 4...Qg5 5.Nxf7! forking queen and rook, but the deeper point: 4...Nxe5?? never happens because after 5.Qh5! threats crash through on f7/e5. The e5-knight capture worked because the f3-knight's move opened the queen's diagonal — a discovery in disguise.

**Coach's tip:** Every time one of your minor pieces stands in front of your queen, rook, or bishop, ask "what happens if it jumps?" You own more discoveries than you play.

**Train it:** Solve 15 discovered-attack puzzles, tagging in each: front piece, rear piece, and both targets. Naming the anatomy builds the scanning habit.
