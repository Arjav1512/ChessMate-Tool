# Skewers

A skewer is a pin reversed: the MORE valuable piece stands in front and must move, exposing the one behind it to capture.

**Why it matters:** Skewers convert small alignment carelessness into won material instantly — and in endgames, the rook skewer behind a promoting pawn decides entire games.

**Recognize it:** Any time two enemy pieces share a rank, file, or diagonal with the bigger one in front, a long-range piece (queen, rook, bishop) may skewer. Checks make the strongest skewers: the king MUST step aside.

**Common mistakes:**
- Aligning your own king and queen (or queen and rook) on one line "temporarily" — one enemy bishop tempo later, the queen is gone.
- Seeing only pins on a line and missing that reversing the attack order wins more.
- In endgames, promoting hastily: with rooks on, always check who skewers whom after the new queen appears.

**How to think:** Treat alignments as hazards and opportunities: each move, scan the long lines for "big piece in front of smaller piece." To create a skewer, use a forcing move to drive the big piece onto the fatal line — a check that herds the king in front of the rook is the classic setup.

**Example:** Endgame fundamental: White pawn on a7, rook behind it; Black rook stops it from a-file... after White's king walks to b7 and the rook checks, Kb8! and if the rook keeps checking, the king hides; but if Black ever grabs the pawn carelessly — Rh8+ then Rh7+ skewers king and rook. The a7/Rh8 skewer geometry wins countless rook endings.

**Coach's tip:** After any queen trade offer or recapture, pause and scan diagonals: "did this line up my king with anything?" Post-trade skewers are the most missed.

**Train it:** Solve 15 skewer puzzles, then set up K+R+pawn-on-7th versus K+R and practice the promotion skewer until you can play it blindfold.
