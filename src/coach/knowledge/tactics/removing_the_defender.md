# Removing the Defender

When a target is defended, attack the defender instead: capture it, chase it away, or overload it until the target falls. Every defended tactic is one removal away from working.

**Why it matters:** This is the master key of combinations. Most "brilliant" sacrifices are just removal operations: the defender is eliminated, and a previously impossible tactic executes.

**Recognize it:** Whenever you find a near-tactic ("Qxf7 would be mate IF the knight weren't guarding f7"), the position is telling you the real target: that knight. Also count defenders versus attackers on key squares — a lone defender of an important square is a removal candidate.

**Common mistakes:**
- Abandoning a promising idea because the target is defended once — one defender is an invitation, not a refusal.
- Removing a defender with a bigger piece it can profitably capture (removal must win the exchange of duties, not lose material).
- Forgetting your OWN overloaded defenders: one piece guarding two squares is a promise you can't fully keep.

**How to think:** Work backward from the wish: "I want to capture X. Who defends X? Can I capture, deflect, pin, or overload that piece?" Chains count too — sometimes you remove the defender of the defender. Each removal step should be forcing (check or capture) so the opponent can't reorganize.

**Example:** Black king g8, rook f8, knight f6 guarding h7 against your Qd3/Bc2 battery. 1.Bxf6! removes the guard; after 1...gxf6 (or 1...Bxf6) 2.Qh7# or a decisive attack follows. The bishop wasn't sacrificed — it was traded for a job.

**Coach's tip:** In your calculations, label every enemy piece with its job ("this knight guards h7 and d5"). Pieces with two jobs are overloaded; pieces with one job are removal targets.

**Train it:** Solve 15 "remove the defender" puzzles, writing down the defender's job before finding the removal. Then in one of your own games, list every defender's duties at move 25.
