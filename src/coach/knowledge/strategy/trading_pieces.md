# Trading Pieces

Every exchange changes the game permanently: trades are irreversible decisions about which pieces remain. Strong players treat each one as a small negotiation — and never trade "just because."

**Why it matters:** Ten to fifteen trades happen per game; each shifts attack potential, defense burden, structure, and endgame class. A player with sound trade judgment gets winning endgames for free from equal middlegames.

**Recognize it:** Before any exchange, identify what each side is really giving: attacker-versus-defender (trades favor the defender), good piece for bad (never volunteer your active piece for their passive one), structure effects (which recapture improves whose pawns), and the endgame the remaining pieces produce.

**Common mistakes:**
- Trading when ahead by ONE rule ("simplify when up material") while ignoring activity — trading into their active rook endgame drew a thousand won games.
- Releasing tension automatically: capturing first often surrenders a lasting plus (the one who takes usually concedes something).
- Trading the defender of a color complex (a fianchetto bishop!) for a jobless piece of equal value — the values were equal, the JOBS were not.

**How to think:** Ask three questions on every offered trade: (1) whose remaining pieces are better after it? (2) does the recapture help my structure or theirs? (3) which endgame family does this steer toward, and who wins that family? If two of three favor you, trade; if none, keep the tension and improve.

**Example:** Up an exchange, you can return it: Rxc6! giving rook for knight when the c6-knight held the whole queenside together. Material count says "even again"; the position says the defense just lost its keystone. Trades are measured in jobs, not points.

**Coach's tip:** When cramped — trade. When attacking — don't. When ahead material — trade PIECES, not pawns. When behind — trade PAWNS, not pieces. Four defaults that are right far more often than not.

**Train it:** Review five of your games and grade every exchange (helped / neutral / hurt) using the three questions above. Most players find one recurring trade error — usually releasing tension too early — within a single session.
