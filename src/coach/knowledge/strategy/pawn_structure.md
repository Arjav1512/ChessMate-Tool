# Pawn Structure

Pawns are the skeleton of the position: they move slowly, never backward, and every advance is permanent. The structure decides which pieces are good, where the play happens, and what both plans must be.

**Why it matters:** Pieces come and go; structure remains. Most "mysterious" grandmaster moves are structure moves — creating a target, fixing a weakness, preparing a break that pays off twenty moves later.

**Recognize it:** Read any position structurally in four steps: find the pawn islands (fewer is better), the weak pawns (isolated, backward, doubled — weak only if attackable), the weak SQUARES (holes no pawn can ever guard), and the pawn breaks available to each side.

**Common mistakes:**
- Making a "natural" pawn advance that permanently concedes a square (…h6 gifting g6, f3 gifting e3).
- Judging doubled pawns as automatically bad — doubled pawns guarding key central squares and opening a file are often strength.
- Playing piece moves in a position that demands a break: when no piece improvement exists, the pawn lever IS the plan.

**How to think:** Before middlegame planning, name your break — the pawn move that changes the structure in your favor (…c5 in the French, b4-b5 in the Carlsbad, f4-f5 in the King's Indian). Every piece should take a job related to it: preparing it, supporting it, or exploiting the lines it opens.

**Example:** In the Carlsbad structure (White pawns a2 b2 c3 d4 vs Black a7 b7 c6 d5), White's minority attack b4–b5xc6 manufactures a permanent weakness on c6 out of thin air. The entire game plan is written in the pawns from move ten.

**Coach's tip:** Before EVERY pawn move ask two questions: which squares does this weaken forever, and can any piece exploit them? Pawn moves are the only unretractable decisions in chess.

**Train it:** Take five of your recent games and, at move 15 of each, write down every pawn island, weakness, and available break for both sides. Structural sight is a listing habit before it becomes intuition.
