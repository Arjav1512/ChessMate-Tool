# Initiative

The initiative is the ability to keep making threats the opponent must answer. Whoever holds it chooses the game's questions; the other side only writes answers.

**Why it matters:** With the initiative, your pieces coordinate toward THEIR weaknesses while theirs babysit yours. Small material deficits routinely lose to a lasting initiative — tempo is a currency you can spend on attack.

**Recognize it:** Ask after each move: who is reacting? If your last three moves answered their threats, you don't have it. Signs you do: their pieces retreat, their pawn moves are defensive, your candidate list is long and theirs is forced.

**Common mistakes:**
- Trading pieces while attacking — every exchange is a gift to the defender.
- Cashing the initiative into a small material gain (winning the exchange, ending the attack) when one more attacking wave was decisive.
- Pausing an attack to "consolidate" — the initiative evaporates in one tempo and the counterattack begins.

**How to think:** Initiative arithmetic: each threat you make must cost them at least one tempo to answer. Chain threats so each answering move worsens their position (forced pawn weaknesses, pieces driven to passive squares). When you can't find another threat, THAT'S the moment to bank profit — not before.

**Example:** A typical gambit line: you're a pawn down but every move — Bc4 hitting f7, Ng5 doubling on it, Qb3 tripling — forces a defensive reply. Six such moves and the defender's pieces stand where THEY had to, not where they wanted; the combination that follows was purchased with those tempi.

**Coach's tip:** When you hold the initiative and see a good move, look for a better one — specifically, a FORCING better one. When you're defending, the mirror advice: any move that makes a real counter-threat is worth two passive defenses.

**Train it:** Play five gambit games (either side of the King's Gambit works) counting consecutive forcing moves. Learning what the initiative FEELS like when it dies teaches you to keep it alive.
