# Weak Squares

A weak square is one that can never again be defended by a pawn. It becomes a permanent home for enemy pieces — an outpost from which they cannot be evicted, only tolerated.

**Why it matters:** Weak squares are forever. Material can be regained and attacks repelled, but a knight installed on d5 or f5 warps every plan for the rest of the game.

**Recognize it:** Trace pawn moves backward: every advance leaves squares no pawn can guard again (…g6 weakens f6/h6; f3 weakens e3/g3). A weakness matters when three things align: the square is advanced (their half of the board or the frontier), an enemy piece — usually a knight — can REACH it, and you cannot trade off the occupier.

**Common mistakes:**
- Counting a hole as weakness when no enemy piece can ever reach it — an unreachable weakness is a ghost.
- Trading away the fianchettoed bishop and only then noticing the color complex it guarded (g7/f6/h6) belongs to the opponent.
- Chasing an outpost knight with pawn moves that create two new holes to evict one occupant.

**How to think:** Think in color complexes, not single squares: weaknesses come in families (all dark squares around a castled king, the c4/d5 light squares in Nimzo structures). To exploit: knight to the hole, trade the defenders of that color, then the infiltration routes open by themselves. To defend: keep the right minor piece (the one matching the weak color) alive at almost any cost.

**Example:** After ...f6?! in many structures, e6 becomes a hole. Watch a strong player: Nf3–d4, pieces trade, Nd4–e6 — the knight sits ON the position's throat, hitting g7/c7/d8/f8 simultaneously. Every Black plan now includes the phrase "...but the e6-knight."

**Coach's tip:** The best time to notice a weak square is BEFORE your pawn move creates it. The second best time is before your opponent's knight is two hops away.

**Train it:** In five master games, pause at move 20 and map each side's weak squares and which piece could occupy them. Then check what actually happened — outpost occupation predicts winners remarkably well.
