# The Bishop Pair

Two bishops against bishop-and-knight or two knights control both color complexes at long range. In open and semi-open positions the pair is worth roughly half a pawn — before anyone does anything.

**Why it matters:** The bishop pair is the most commonly available long-term advantage: opponents hand it over in routine trades. Knowing when it matters (and when it doesn't) guides dozens of exchange decisions every game.

**Recognize it:** You hold the pair whenever they've traded a bishop for your knight. Its power scales with openness: mobile pawns and open diagonals amplify it; locked chains mute it. The pair's signature strength: you can always trade ONE bishop at the perfect moment — the opponent never chooses which.

**Common mistakes:**
- Giving up a bishop for a knight without gaining structure, time, or material ("it was equal-ish" is a small loss, repeated forever).
- Owning the pair and locking the pawns anyway — bishops need the position their owner must provide.
- Facing the pair passively: its antidotes are concrete — fix pawns on the right colors, plant knights on protected outposts, trade one bishop off.

**How to think:** With the pair: open the position gradually (pawn breaks that don't create knight outposts), stretch play across both wings — bishops commute, knights don't — and delay committing pawns. Against the pair: close lines, create ONE unassailable knight outpost, and hunt the more dangerous bishop for exchange.

**Example:** Classic technique: in a symmetrical queenless middlegame, the pair's owner advances pawns on BOTH wings a move at a time. Each advance asks the short-range knight to re-route; by the time pawns fix, the knight has no square left on either side. Fischer converted exactly this dozens of times.

**Coach's tip:** Evaluate every minor-piece trade with one question: "who ends up with the pair, and does this pawn structure feed it or starve it?" That question alone plays hundreds of moves correctly.

**Train it:** Play five training games starting from an open position with bishops-vs-bishop+knight (remove a knight and bishop symmetrically). Win with the pair; then swap sides and hold.
