# Piece Activity

An active piece attacks, defends, and controls squares that matter; a passive one occupies space. Activity is the most convertible advantage in chess — it becomes tactics, attacks, and endgame wins.

**Why it matters:** Equal material with unequal activity is not equal. Engines' evaluations track piece activity more closely than any human-visible factor; "I was fine, then suddenly lost" almost always means activity had already tilted.

**Recognize it:** Piece-by-piece audit: count meaningful squares each piece influences. Knights want advanced protected outposts; bishops want open diagonals and mobile pawn structures; rooks want open or half-open files (and the 7th rank); the queen wants everyone else placed first.

**Common mistakes:**
- Improving your best piece again while your worst piece rots (the classic: three moves with the good knight, zero with the c8-bishop).
- Winning a pawn at the cost of total passivity — the material converts only if you survive the squeeze.
- Placing pieces where they're "developed" but jobless: a bishop staring at its own pawn chain is furniture.

**How to think:** When no forcing play exists, run the worst-piece algorithm: identify your least active piece, find its dream square, and start the journey. Do this every quiet move and your positions improve on autopilot. The mirror skill: notice their worst piece and deny its route.

**Example:** A rook lift (Re1–e3–g3) transforms a defensive rook into a kingside attacker in two moves. Nothing was captured, no weakness created — yet the attack often decides right there, because attacking force is counted in ACTIVE pieces only.

**Coach's tip:** In level positions, the player who makes the last piece improvement before contact usually wins the tactics that follow. Patience with placement is a tactical weapon.

**Train it:** Play three games where every quiet move must answer "which piece did this activate?" — write the piece's name on your scoresheet. Jobless moves become visible immediately.
