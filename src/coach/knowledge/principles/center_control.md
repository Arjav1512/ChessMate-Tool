# Center Control

The four central squares (d4, d5, e4, e5) are the high ground of the board. Pieces placed there, or controlling them from afar, reach both wings quickly; pieces shut out of the center arrive late to every fight.

**Why it matters:** A knight on e5 attacks eight squares; on h1 it attacks two. Whoever rules the center moves pieces between wings faster, which decides most attacks and defenses.

**Recognize it:** Ask who would win if all four central squares were occupied by each side's pawns and pieces. Also watch for the moment a stable center becomes mobile — central pawn breaks change everything.

**Common mistakes:**
- Believing the center must be occupied by pawns — controlling it with pieces (hypermodern style, like the Pirc or Nimzo-Indian) is equally valid.
- Grabbing wing pawns while the opponent builds a pawn duo on e4/d4.
- Launching a flank attack while the center is open — the classic recipe for being counterpunched through the middle.

**How to think:** Before any wing action, check the center: a closed or stable center permits a flank attack; an open or tense one demands central play first. "Meet a flank attack with a break in the center" is one of the most reliable rules in chess.

**Example:** In the King's Indian, Black allows White a big center with d4/e4, then strikes it with ...e5 and ...f5. Both sides obey the same principle — the fight is about the center even when the pawns come from the side.

**Coach's tip:** When you don't know what to do, improve your control of a central square. It is rarely wrong and often quietly winning.

**Train it:** Play through one classic game (try Capablanca) counting, every five moves, how many of the four central squares each side controls.
