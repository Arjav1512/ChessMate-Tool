# French Defense (1.e4 e6)

The French is a counterattacking system: Black concedes space and a temporarily bad bishop for a rock-solid structure and clear, thematic counterplay against White's pawn chain.

**Why it matters:** The French teaches the deepest structural lesson in chess — how to attack a pawn chain at its base. Players who understand it stop fearing cramped positions.

**Recognize it:** After 1.e4 e6 2.d4 d5, White chooses: 3.e5 (Advance — the chain d4/e5 forms), 3.Nc3 or 3.Nd2 (tension), or 3.exd5 (Exchange). Black's play revolves around the breaks ...c5 and later ...f6.

**Common mistakes:**
- Forgetting the c8-bishop: if you never plan ...b6/...Ba6 or a trade, it stays a spectator all game.
- Playing ...f6 too early, opening the king before development justifies it.
- As White, defending d4 passively instead of using the space for a kingside initiative.

**How to think:** Attack the chain at its base: against d4/e5, the base is d4, so ...c5 and piece pressure (…Nc6, …Qb6) come first; ...f6 hits the head later. White's logic mirrors it — protect d4, then use the e5 space to attack the king.

**Example:** Advance French: 1.e4 e6 2.d4 d5 3.e5 c5 4.c3 Nc6 5.Nf3 Qb6 — every Black move bites on d4. If White defends carelessly (6.Be2 cxd4 7.cxd4 Nxd4!), the base falls to a standard tactic (8.Nxd4 Qxd4?? fails to 9.Bb5+ winning the queen — check why!).

**Coach's tip:** In the French, your worst piece defines your game. Solve the c8-bishop's future in the first ten moves, even if the solution is trading it.

**Train it:** From the Advance structure, list every way Black can add pressure to d4 — then every way White can defend it. The side with the longer list usually stands better.
