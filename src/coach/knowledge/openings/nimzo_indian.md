# Nimzo-Indian Defense (1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 Bb4)

The Nimzo-Indian controls e4 with pieces, not pawns: the bishop pins the c3-knight, and Black happily trades it to saddle White with doubled c-pawns.

**Why it matters:** It is the highest-regarded defense to 1.d4 — a masterclass in the bishop-pair-versus-structure bargain that defines modern positional chess.

**Recognize it:** The pin on c3 is the trademark. White's main tries: 4.e3 (flexible), 4.Qc2 (avoiding doubled pawns at the cost of a tempo), 4.f3 (grabbing the center by force). Black's levers: ...c5, ...d5, or the light-square blockade with ...b6 and ...Ne4.

**Common mistakes:**
- As Black, trading on c3 without extracting something (doubled pawns, a tempo, or the better structure) — the bishop pair given away for nothing is just worse.
- Missing the follow-up plan after ...Bxc3: the doubled pawns only matter if Black blockades c4/e4 and attacks c4 with ...Na5/...Ba6.
- As White, winning the bishop pair then opening the position too late — locked centers make the extra bishop a spectator.

**How to think:** Every Nimzo decision weighs two currencies: structure versus bishops. Black's compass: light squares. If you own e4 and c4, your position plays itself. White's compass: open the game for the bishops before the weaknesses fix themselves permanently.

**Example:** Sämisch structure: 4.a3 Bxc3+ 5.bxc3 c5 6.e3 Nc6 7.Bd3 O-O 8.Ne2 b6 9.O-O Ba6! — Black immediately targets the c4-pawn, the fixed cost of White's bishop pair. Games hinge on whether White's center and bishops become active before c4 falls.

**Coach's tip:** In Nimzo structures, one blockading knight on e4 or c4 is worth the bishop pair. Fight for those squares like they're material.

**Train it:** Play the Sämisch structure from both sides (five games each): as Black besiege c4; as White launch e4/f3–f4. You'll internalize both halves of the bargain.
