# Catalan Opening (1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.g3)

The Catalan crosses the Queen's Gambit with a kingside fianchetto: the g2-bishop rakes the long diagonal, turning White's opening advantage into permanent, low-risk pressure.

**Why it matters:** It is the modern elite's favorite squeeze — perfect for players who want lasting pull without forcing theory. Facing it well is mandatory for every ...e6 defender.

**Recognize it:** White's setup: d4, c4, g3, Bg2, Nf3, O-O. The defining tension: Black may take c4, but the g2-bishop plus Qc2/a4 usually recovers the pawn with interest. Open Catalan: ...dxc4; Closed: Black holds d5 and suffers pleasantly.

**Common mistakes:**
- As Black, keeping the c4-pawn at all costs (...b5?!) — the resulting queenside holes feed the g2-bishop forever.
- Freeing with ...c5 too late; the timely break is Black's only real equalizer.
- As White, rushing to regain c4 while Black develops — the pawn is an investment; collect it with tempo or not at all.

**How to think:** The long diagonal is the plot: every exchange or pawn move is judged by whether it opens or blocks g2–a8. Black's counter-strategies: block it (...c6, ...Nd5), trade it (...Bb7 after securing ...b6), or outrun it (quick ...c5 and piece activity).

**Example:** Open main line: 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.g3 d5 4.Bg2 dxc4 5.Nf3 Be7 6.O-O O-O 7.Qc2 a6 8.a4 — White calmly prepares Qxc4 while a4 stops ...b5. Black got a free tempo but White's bishop hits b7 for the next forty moves.

**Coach's tip:** Endgames flatter the Catalan bishop. As Black, trade queens only when your queenside is already solved; as White, invite every ending.

**Train it:** Play three Catalan endgames (queens off, g2-bishop vs c8-bishop) from both sides — you'll learn why grandmasters grab the White side.
