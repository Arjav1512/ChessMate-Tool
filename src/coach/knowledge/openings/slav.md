# Slav Defense (1.d4 d5 2.c4 c6)

The Slav defends the Queen's Gambit with ...c6 instead of ...e6, holding d5 while keeping the c8-bishop's diagonal open — solidity without the QGD's problem piece.

**Why it matters:** It is one of the soundest defenses to 1.d4 at every level, and its structures (the ...dxc4 grab, the Semi-Slav triangle) run through modern top-level chess.

**Recognize it:** Pure Slav: ...c6, ...Nf6, then often ...dxc4 followed by ...Bf5 and ...e6 with the bishop already out (Czech Slav: 4.Nc3 dxc4 5.a4 Bf5). Semi-Slav: ...e6 comes early, accepting the closed bishop in exchange for the dynamic ...dxc4+...b5 plans (Meran) or the wild Botvinnik lines.

**Common mistakes:**
- Confusing the two families: in the pure Slav the bishop MUST get out before ...e6; in the Semi-Slav keeping it in is a deliberate dynamic bargain.
- As Black, holding the c4-pawn with ...b5 without knowing the a4/axb5 punishments.
- As White, playing the position like a QGD Carlsbad — cxd5 here gives Black the easy ...cxd5 recapture and a fine game.

**How to think:** The pawn tension c4/d5 defines the play. Black asks: take on c4 (and fight to keep development flowing while White regains it) or hold the fort with ...e6? White asks: force the pace with e4 ideas, or clamp with e3 and slow expansion?

**Example:** Czech main line: 1.d4 d5 2.c4 c6 3.Nf3 Nf6 4.Nc3 dxc4 5.a4 Bf5 6.e3 e6 7.Bxc4 Bb4 8.O-O — Black has developed every piece to its best square and merely needs ...O-O and ...c5 to stand fully equal. That ...c5 break is the Slav's whole endgame promise.

**Coach's tip:** In every Slav structure, ...c5 (or ...e5) is the freeing move. If you cannot name which one your position wants, you don't have a plan yet.
