# Italian Game (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4)

The Italian develops the bishop to its most natural diagonal, eyeing f7, and fights for the center with c3 and d4 — classical chess in its purest form.

**Why it matters:** It is the ideal first opening: every idea (fast development, central breaks, f7 pressure) is a transferable principle rather than a memorized trick.

**Recognize it:** The bishop on c4 marks it. Modern treatment is the "Giuoco Pianissimo": d3, c3, Re1, Nbd2–f1–g3, a slow build where plans beat memorization. Sharp alternatives: 4.Ng5 against the Two Knights, or an early d4.

**Common mistakes:**
- Falling for (or missing) the f7/f2 tactics that define the first ten moves — the Fried Liver geography.
- Playing c3 then blocking the d4 break with Nc3-style development — the two don't mix.
- Trading the c4-bishop for a knight without gain; it is usually your best attacker.

**How to think:** White's long-term question is where the d-pawn stops: d3 (slow, maneuvering) or d4 (open, tactical). Black equalizes by matching central breaks — a well-timed ...d5 solves most problems.

**Example:** 3...Nf6 4.Ng5 d5! 5.exd5 — now 5...Nxd5? invites 6.Nxf7! (the Fried Liver), while 5...Na5 (the Polerio) is the disciplined path, giving a pawn temporarily for the bishop pair and the initiative.

**Coach's tip:** In quiet Italian positions the winner is usually whoever reroutes a knight to f5/f4 (or ...f4 for Black) first. Slow does not mean planless.

**Train it:** Play the position after 4.d3 Bc5 5.c3 ten times, alternating colors, with one goal: execute your thematic break (d4 or ...d5) under the best conditions you can arrange.
