# Queen's Gambit (1.d4 d5 2.c4)

The Queen's Gambit offers a wing pawn to deflect Black's central d-pawn — not a real sacrifice (the pawn is always recoverable) but a fight for central control on positional terms.

**Why it matters:** QGD structures — the Carlsbad, the IQP, hanging pawns — are the vocabulary of all closed openings. Learn them here and half of 1.d4 chess becomes familiar.

**Recognize it:** Declined (2...e6): solid, with the c8-bishop as Black's project. Accepted (2...dxc4): Black concedes the center for free development and a later ...c5. Slav (2...c6): defends d5 while keeping the bishop's diagonal open.

**Common mistakes:**
- As Black in the QGA, trying to keep the c4-pawn with ...b5 — the classic 3.e3/4.a4 punishment recovers it with interest.
- As White, releasing the tension with cxd5 automatically, giving Black easy equality unless the Carlsbad plan is intended.
- Ignoring the minority attack: in Carlsbad structures, b4–b5 is White's whole queenside plan and Black must prepare against it early.

**How to think:** The central tension d5/c4 is an asset — whoever resolves it should get something for it. Ask what structure each capture leaves: cxd5 exd5 gives the Carlsbad (minority attack vs kingside play); ...dxc4 gives White a center and Black the c-file counterplay.

**Example:** Carlsbad plan: after 1.d4 d5 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 Nf6 4.cxd5 exd5 5.Bg5 Be7 6.e3 c6, White plays Bd3, Qc2, and eventually b4–b5xc6, creating a permanent weakness on c6 that wins endgames forty moves later.

**Coach's tip:** In the QGD, Black's game is fine exactly when the c8-bishop is; every plan should serve that bishop's future.

**Train it:** From the Carlsbad structure, play the minority attack as White against an engine set to defend. Land b5 under good conditions three times — the technique will stay with you.
