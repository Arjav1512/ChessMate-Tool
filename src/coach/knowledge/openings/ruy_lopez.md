# Ruy Lopez (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5)

The Ruy Lopez applies indirect pressure: the bishop pins nothing yet, but the standing threat of Bxc6 followed by Nxe5 forces Black to spend moves justifying the e5-pawn — and those small debts compound.

**Why it matters:** The Lopez is chess's longest-running masterclass in converting a tiny, permanent initiative. Studying it teaches maneuvering better than any other opening.

**Recognize it:** White's plans are timeless: c3 + d4 for a big center, the "Spanish knight" tour Nb1–d2–f1–g3, kingside expansion. Black's counters: ...b5 and ...Na5 hitting the bishop, the solid Berlin (3...Nf6), or open lines with an early ...Nxe4.

**Common mistakes:**
- As Black, releasing the central tension (...exd4) without need, handing White a free hand.
- As White, exchanging on c6 without a concrete reason — the bishop pair usually outweighs the doubled pawns.
- Both sides: pushing pawns in front of their own king during the long maneuvering phase.

**How to think:** The Lopez rewards patience: improve your worst piece, keep the tension, and only open the position when your pieces stand better. Ask before every trade: does this help my structure or theirs?

**Example:** Main line: 3...a6 4.Ba4 Nf6 5.O-O Be7 6.Re1 b5 7.Bb3 d6 8.c3 O-O 9.h3 — nine moves of pure preparation. h3 stops ...Bg4, so d4 comes next without the annoying pin.

**Coach's tip:** In closed Spanish positions, knights outrank bishops until the position opens. Route yours to f5 (or ...f4) before touching pawns.

**Train it:** Study one Karpov or Carlsen Ruy Lopez win. Write down each piece's route from start square to its final, best square — you'll see the "Spanish tour" everywhere afterward.
