# King's Indian Defense (1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 g6)

The King's Indian concedes the classical center on purpose: Black builds a fortress with ...g6/...Bg7/...d6, invites White to overextend, then detonates the position with ...e5 and ...f5.

**Why it matters:** It is the sharpest strategic bargain in chess — structural risk for attacking chances — and it teaches the flank-versus-center dynamic better than any opening.

**Recognize it:** The classical tabiya: 3.Nc3 Bg7 4.e4 d6 5.Nf3 O-O 6.Be2 e5 7.O-O Nc6 8.d5 Ne7 — the center locks, and the famous race begins: Black storms the kingside with ...f5–f4, ...g5–g4; White breaks through with c5 and b4 on the queenside.

**Common mistakes:**
- As Black, trading the g7-bishop or blocking it permanently — it is the soul of the defense, both attacker and bodyguard.
- Playing wing pawns before the center is fixed; the ...f5 break only works when White cannot open the middle in reply.
- As White, joining Black's kingside race instead of playing your faster queenside break — you win the KID by being quicker on YOUR wing.

**How to think:** Once the center locks, the evaluation is a race question: count tempi to each side's breakthrough. Every move must serve your break or slow theirs; a neutral move is a lost tempo in a sprint.

**Example:** In the Mar del Plata (9.Ne1 Nd7 10.Be3 f5 11.f3 f4 12.Bf2 g5), Black's pawns march at the king while White's c5/b4/c6 arrives on the other side. Games are decided by two or three tempi — which is why theory runs thirty moves deep.

**Coach's tip:** As the KID player, learn the sacrifice patterns (...Nf4, ...Bh3, the ...g3 break). As the anti-KID player, remember: trade dark-squared bishops and Black's attack usually dies.

**Train it:** Set up the locked Mar del Plata center and play only the pawn races (both sides, no retreats allowed) five times. You'll learn tempo-counting viscerally.
