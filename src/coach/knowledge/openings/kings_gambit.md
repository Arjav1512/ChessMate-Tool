# King's Gambit (1.e4 e5 2.f4)

The King's Gambit offers a real pawn for open lines and a lead in development — the romantic era's main weapon, still dangerous whenever Black plays timidly.

**Why it matters:** No opening teaches initiative-versus-material more honestly. Even if you never play it, defending against gambits correctly (develop, return material at the right moment) is a core skill it forces you to learn.

**Recognize it:** After 2...exf4 (Accepted), White aims for d4, Bc4/Nf3, and a quick strike at f7 before Black consolidates the extra pawn. Declined setups (2...Bc5 hitting g1–a7, or the counter-gambit 2...d5) fight for the initiative instead of the pawn.

**Common mistakes:**
- As White, regaining the f4-pawn at the cost of the initiative — the pawn was the ticket price, not the prize.
- As Black, greedily keeping f4 with ...g5 while ignoring development; one d4/h4 break and the kingside caves.
- Both sides: castling into it — check which side of the board the open f-file punishes before choosing where the king lives.

**How to think:** Count tempi, not pawns. White must convert the lead into an attack within ~10 moves or stand simply worse. Black's golden rule against every gambit: develop first, return the pawn at the moment it buys full equality (often ...d5).

**Example:** Modern defense: 1.e4 e5 2.f4 exf4 3.Nf3 d5! 4.exd5 Nf6 — Black gives the pawn back instantly for free development and a clean structure, sidestepping a century of attacking theory. This "anti-greed" recipe defuses most gambits.

**Coach's tip:** If you accept a gambit, set a timer in your head: every move you spend not developing is interest paid on borrowed material.

**Train it:** Play the Accepted from both sides in blitz — as White land an attack before move 15; as Black practice the return-the-pawn equalizer. Both lessons generalize far beyond this opening.
