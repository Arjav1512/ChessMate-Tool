# Time Management

The clock is a piece: time is the resource you spend to buy accuracy, and both hoarding it and burning it lose games that the board alone would not.

**Why it matters:** More club games are decided by clock catastrophe than by opening knowledge — a winning position with ten seconds is not a winning position. Managing time IS managing blunder risk.

**Recognize it:** Your danger patterns are personal but typical: burning ten minutes on move nine in known territory, thinking longest exactly when tired, matching the opponent's blitz pace in a slow game, or "saving" time so well you never calculated anything. Post-game, compare your clock graph to your mistakes — they correlate.

**Common mistakes:**
- Spending big time on forced or near-equal choices ("which recapture" when both are fine) and then blitzing the genuinely critical moment.
- Refusing to move until certainty arrives — chess rarely offers certainty; it offers good-enough plus a ticking clock.
- Entering time trouble with a complicated position when a simplifying (even slightly worse) line was available.

**How to think:** Budget by move type: openings you know — seconds; routine improving moves — under a minute; CRITICAL moments (captures that change structure, attack/defense commitments, forced-line entries) — real time, this is what you saved it for. Learn to recognize criticality: irreversibility is the signal. In time trouble: simplify, prefer solid over sharp, and run the one-question blunder check every move without exception.

**Example:** You have 40 minutes for 20 moves in a sharp middlegame. The discipline: identify the two or three genuinely critical decisions coming (their kingside break, your d5 push) and pre-allocate ten minutes each — everything else plays on rhythm. Players who do this feel "lucky" in time scrambles; it isn't luck.

**Coach's tip:** If you're a chronic burner, adopt a hard rule: no move over five minutes before move 25 unless material is hanging. If you're a chronic blitzer: sit on your hands and write the opponent's threat down before each reply.

**Train it:** Play three games with your clock times noted per move, then review WHERE the time went versus where the mistakes happened. One session of this changes habits faster than advice ever does.
